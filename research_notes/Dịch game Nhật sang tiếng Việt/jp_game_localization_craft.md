# The craft of translating/localizing Japanese games (VNs and story-heavy gacha): line-level techniques, the literal-vs-localized debate, style guides, and good/bad renderings

Scope note: these notes cover JP->EN craft as written up by professional localizers (Sega/Atlus, Capcom, NIS America, 8-4, Cygames), fan-TL commentary, and academic work. Vietnamese conventions, Blue Archive, tools and models are left to the other researcher. Tags used: **[Consensus]** = several independent practitioners or sources agree; **[Opinion]** = one practitioner's or one critic's view; **[Contested]** = sources openly disagree. "Snippet-only" means the page could not be fetched (403 or truncated) and the claim comes from a search-result excerpt, so confidence is lower.

---

## Q1. How do experienced JP game/VN translators handle the specific features (honorifics, keigo, particles, pronouns, verbal tics, dialect, SFX, puns, names/terms, TL notes, line length, ellipses/stutters, 「」)?

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree that the Japanese markers that signal character (pronouns, particles, role language, dialect, keigo) mostly can't be carried over word for word into English. They get "flattened", and the translator has to "re-inflate" them through word choice, syntax, contractions, punctuation and typography, reproducing each marker's function rather than its form. Honorifics are the main exception: in Japan-set stories the trend since about 2015 is to keep them (hybrid foreignization), while fantasy or non-Japan settings tend to swap in English forms of address.

### Cited Findings

**Honorifics (-san/-chan/-kun/-sama/senpai/aniki)**
- [Consensus, academic] *Yakuza 0*'s English subtitles keep -san, -sama, -han, -chan and -kun in Latin script, but switch names to Western order. Keigo endings (desu/masu) are almost never carried over. The authors call this a hybrid of foreignizing (keeping honorifics) and domesticating (name order), quote "There are no pure domestications, as there are no pure foreignizations", and read it as a sign that Anglo-American audiences now tolerate more foreignness. Function-level readings: Kiryu's -san for his superior Kazama shows "submissiveness and respect". -han is Majima's Osaka-dialect form, so keeping it preserves both a relationship and a regional marker. Sagawa calling Majima -chan hints at power harassment or affection. A hostess calling a client -kun shows a "friendly facade". — [Santos & Humblé 2023, Cadernos de Tradução 43(1)](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/7319/731976434018/movil/)
- [Opinion -> later reversed] Sega/Atlus first rendered *aniki* as "sir", which drew criticism. *Yakuza 6* kept "aniki" and added a loading-screen tooltip: "Aniki means big bro, but in the yakuza it's a term of respect." "Senpai" and "Kiryu-aniki" are used as-is because Western audiences now know them. — [Game Developer, Strichart Q&A, 5 Feb 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- [Consensus, rule of thumb] Honorific policy "may vary from publisher to publisher", and the call is usually left to the translator and editor. The common heuristic is to omit honorifics in stories set outside Japan and keep them in Japan-set stories where relationships matter. — [Anime News Network, Answerman, 28 Sep 2018](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/answerman/2018-09-28/.137444) (snippet-only)
- [Practice, VN publisher] For the fantasy eroge *Funbag Fantasy 2*, MangaGamer's translators chose proper English forms of address instead of fixed equivalents for each Japanese honorific. — [MangaGamer blog, 18 Nov 2019](https://blog.mangagamer.org/2019/11/18/honorifics-and-titles-in-funbag-fantasy-2/) (snippet-only, 403). An older 2011 MangaGamer comment thread describes honorifics being removed while cultural references stayed, which was house practice at the time (outside the date range; historical context only). — [MangaGamer blog, 2011](https://mangagamer.wordpress.com/2011/10/07/ef-the-first-tale-updates/) (snippet-only)
- [Contested, fan opinion] A VN-community article argues that the strongest case is to keep honorifics when a story is culturally rooted in Japan, because they carry relational information English can't encode. It adds that dropping them "isn't automatically laziness", but then tone or narration has to signal relationship shifts instead. — [VN Paths, undated, ~2024–26](https://vnpaths.com/should-honorifics-be-kept-in-visual-novel-translations/) (snippet-only). In a Novel Updates reader poll, the top option was "Keep every honorific, don't translate most 'weeb' words" at 37.2%, with the other options close behind, which shows a split readership. — [Novel Updates forum](https://www.novelupdatesforum.com/threads/use-of-honorifics-in-translated-works.103248/) (snippet-only, undated)
- [Community style guide, gacha] The fan-run Umamusume Wiki keeps honorifics hyphenated ("Spe-chan"), but renders senpai/kouhai as "upperclassman/underclassman" and Onii/Onee-chan as "Brother/Sister". It puts given names first ("Tazuna Hayakawa") and avoids gendered pronouns for the player character ("Trainer"). — [Umamusume Wiki: Contributing/Guidelines](https://umamusu.wiki/Umamusume_Wiki:Contributing/Guidelines)
- [Academic] A study of contemporary subtitling of Japanese games (it discusses ex-yakuza Kaito, i.e. the *Judgment* series) finds that "both versions retain the use of Japanese honorific suffixes such as -san, -chan, -kun", and that Kaito's rough, masculine-coded speech is carried into the English. — [ResearchGate: "Japanese videogames in English translation: a study of contemporary subtitling practices"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362913669_Japanese_videogames_in_English_translation_a_study_of_contemporary_subtitling_practices) (snippet-only, year not confirmed)

**Keigo / politeness and register**
- [Consensus] Japanese stacks hierarchical politeness signals that "sound awkward in English". For example, English speakers don't say "I humbly accept" when a superior gives them a task. — [Game Informer, 24 Nov 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- [Technique, NIS America] For the elder Roselia in *Trails of Cold Steel*, the team tried an "old-timey accent", dropped it after hearing it read aloud, and instead limited her contractions in casual scenes and removed them entirely in formal ones. Contraction density works as a register dial. — Eric Budensiek (NISA lead editor), [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- Name order and keigo: *Yakuza 0* rarely keeps desu/masu forms and expresses politeness through English phrasing, keeping honorific suffixes as the visible marker. — [Santos & Humblé 2023](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/7319/731976434018/movil/)

**First-person pronouns as characterisation**
- [Consensus, reference] Clyde Mandelin's catalogue of pronoun connotations: watashi is neutral (slightly feminine when informal). Watakushi is formal, and in fiction can read as prim, cultured or snobby. Atakushi suggests a "snooty rich lady". Boku is soft-masculine, "boyishness, gentleness, timidness" (girls using it read as tomboys). Ore is "stronger, harsher, and manlier", crude in formal settings and familiar among friends. Ore-sama is cocky or egotistical (typical of rude villains). Atashi is casual-feminine. Uchi is Kansai-flavoured and young-female. Washi is an old man. Ora is rural, "country bumpkin". Oira is a lovable rascal or mascot. Wagahai is self-important and old-fashioned. Warawa is a condescending noblewoman. Yo is royal. Atai is a rough, brash female. Sessha/soregashi are fictional samurai/ninja pronouns. Jibun is military, police or sports. Mī is a quirky or foreign character. — [Legends of Localization, "Tricky Translations #4", 1 Jun 2019](https://legendsoflocalization.com/articles/personal-pronouns-in-japanese/); ore-sama for villains — [LoL FFVI comparison pt. 4](https://legendsoflocalization.com/comparisons/final-fantasy-vi/part-04/) (snippet-only)
- [Consensus] English has only "I/me", so translation "flattens" these cues, and the core of localization is to "re-inflate" them by other means. Mandelin recommends giving translators speaker details or pre-specified pronouns to avoid flattening and inconsistency. — [LoL 2019](https://legendsoflocalization.com/articles/personal-pronouns-in-japanese/)
- Examples of re-inflation (from reader comments on that article, lower confidence): Guzma's ore-sama (*Pokémon Sun/Moon*) comes across through "it's ya boy Guzma". The royal *yo* is often rendered as the royal "we", a choice a *Katamari Damacy* localizer (Agnes Kaku) described for the King of All Cosmos. Sessō (monk) is often rendered as "this humble servant of [deity]". — [LoL comments, 2019](https://legendsoflocalization.com/articles/personal-pronouns-in-japanese/)
- [Vendor practice] Without context, translators can make tough characters sound feminine or let characters "randomly switch between masculine and feminine speech". Boku vs atashi choices flag personality, age and status. — attributed by search summary to one of [GTE Localize](https://gtelocalize.net/japanese-game-translation/) / [Superjump](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/unlocking-global-markets-a-guide-to-localizing-game-characters/) (vendor blogs; not individually verified)

**Role language (役割語 yakuwarigo) and verbal tics**
- [Academic] Satoshi Kinsui coined yakuwarigo in 2003 for fictional speech styles that index age, gender and class, often unlike real speech. Example markers: boy/tomboy uses boku + ~no sa. Girl uses atashi + ~wa. Noblewoman uses watakushi + ~desu wa / ~orimasu. Samurai uses sessha + ~ja/~oru (gozaru evokes ninja or samurai). Elder doctor (hakase) uses washi + ~ja/~oru. Kansai speaker uses wate + ~ya (tied to comedians, merchants, gangsters). Country person uses ora + ~nda. "Chinese" stereotype uses ~aru. It's hard to translate because it relies on pronouns and sentence-final particles that are "static or absent" in English. — [Wikipedia: Yakuwarigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakuwarigo), citing Teshigawara & Kinsui (2012) and Heerink (2018)
- [Academic opinion] A Leiden MA thesis (Heerink 2018, "Yakuwarigo Lost in Translation") concludes that full translation of role language isn't achievable, but characters can still sound like their personalities. It recommends compensating through typography, punctuation and word choice. — [Leiden thesis](https://studenttheses.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2604481/view) (snippet-only)
- [Opinion, learner source] Catchphrase tics such as だってばよ carry character voice, not literal meaning, so the translator should reproduce the function rather than the words. An invented English tic also tends to be applied inconsistently because no English ending does the same job. — [kodack.net](https://www.kodack.net/en/famous-anime-catchphrases/)
- Example: Naruto's dub rendered だってばよ as "Believe it!", partly to match mouth movements, later dropped it, and used "ya know" for Kushina's variant. — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/what-does-dattebayo-mean-in-naruto/) (fan-level sourcing; details vary between sources)
- Example (weak source): *FFVI*'s Cyan (samurai-style ござる speech) was rendered with archaic "thee/thou" English, which a trope wiki calls a poor fit. — [Tropedia: Verbal Tic](https://tropedia.fandom.com/wiki/Verbal_Tic)

**Sentence-final particles (ね/よ/etc.)**
- [Linguistics] ね asks for confirmation or agreement about shared knowledge. よ asserts something the speaker assumes only they know. ね and よね are usually translated as tag questions ("isn't it?", "right?"). — [Jun Xu, UW–Madison thesis on よね](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/LQDBBOVKMPZJJ8U/R/file-e85ed.pdf)
- [Opinion, translator-blogger] "ne" usually doesn't appear as a word in the English sentence. Its effect shows up through tone, which "is just as important as what is being said". — [Self-Taught Japanese, 5 Oct 2017](https://selftaughtjapanese.com/2017/10/05/japanese-emotive-sentence-ending-particles-ne-na-naa-yo-%E3%81%AD%E3%80%81%E3%81%AA%E3%80%81%E3%81%AA%E3%81%82%E3%80%81%E3%82%88/)
- No professional game-localizer style guide on particles was found (see Gaps). The practitioner rule set below is inferred.

**Dialect (Kansai-ben, Tosa-ben, rural)**
- [Consensus at Sega] "There's no singular way to localise" Kansai-ben, because any English accent (such as the Southern US accent *Ace Attorney* uses for some Kansai speakers) is also tied to one real place. Strichart says he "made a point to overhaul Majima's accent" starting with the *Yakuza* titles he worked on. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/how-quality-localisation-makes-the-yakuza-series-great/) (snippet-only, quote truncated)
- [Practice, Sega 2023] Avoid "making a direct analog between specific English and Japanese dialects". The team has failed if "players are categorically hearing all Kansai speakers with a twang." Tosa-ben (Ryoma, *Ishin!*) was written to read as rustic and insular without mapping to a specific English variety. — Marilyn Lee, [Kotaku, 24 Mar 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- [Contested, gacha 2025–26] As summarized from slides by a critic, Cygames' *Umamusume* EN renders Tamamo Cross's Kansai speech with non-standard spellings ("dis") meant to mimic rhythm and phonetics without using a real English dialect. Yukino Bijin's rural Iwate speech becomes 1930s–60s American idiom ("okey dokey", "in the soup"). Agnes Digital's otaku speech becomes online-fandom slang, and Daitaku Helios's gyaru speech becomes Gen-Z slang as a "parallel culture". The critic calls this "cultural substitution". — [Localizers Exposed, 15 Aug 2026](https://localizers.ch/column/when-localization-stops-translating-what-cygames-cedec-2026-umamusume-presentation-actually-shows/) (an advocacy site opposed to liberal localization; Cygames' own slides were not seen directly)

**Puns and wordplay**
- [Consensus] Puns get rebuilt, not translated. *Yakuza*'s 布団が吹っ飛んだ ("the futon flew away") pun became "How do you avoid dangerous cults? Practice safe sects!" Translators flag puns line by line for editors to rework. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west); Japanese puns "never translate", so teams brainstorm together (e.g. whole afternoons spent on chicken names). — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- Wordplay puzzles get re-engineered: in *Ace Attorney: Spirit of Justice*, the English karuta cards spell "WHET NO 4", which decodes to a different message. — Janet Hsu (Capcom), [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization). In fan-TL guidance, wordplay "need[s] to be recreated entirely in the target language". — [VN Paths guide](https://vnpaths.com/how-to-translate-a-visual-novel/)
- Pac-Man's ghost names were reworked into English names that keep each ghost's personality ("Shadow", "Speedy", "Bashful"), a classic case of compensation. — [ResearchGate: "Found in Translation" (Mangiron)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348781368_Found_in_Translation_Evolving_Approaches_for_the_Localization_of_Japanese_Video_Games) (snippet via search summary; attribution between the listed papers not confirmed)

**Cultural terms, glosses and TL notes**
- [Practice] Instead of footnotes, Sega explains culture *in-game*: a loading-screen tooltip for aniki, added explanatory text for "Yankee" culture, "stadium jumper" glossed as a varsity-jacket-like item, and "sauce face vs soy-sauce face" explained in a crossword. They also check with developers whether a term is literal (e.g. "Shangri-la" in *Yakuza 6* was confirmed as figurative). — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west); *Ishin!* added an updated glossary and a "memoir" feature for its 1867 setting. — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- Familiarity calls get made case by case: tonkotsu ramen vs "pork broth ramen" (Strichart). Momotaro's peach-birth can map to the stork myth when the intent is "where babies come from" (Chavez). — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- [Academic] Fan groups use TL notes both to solve problems and to address readers directly, which makes the translator visible. Notes are rare in professional comics translation and are a hallmark of scanlation. — [Fabbretti 2016, Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/96398/); [Translation & Interpreting Studies journal, "The Use of Translation Notes in Manga Scanlation"](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/tc/index.php/TC/article/view/28489)
- [Academic] O'Hagan's (2008) fan-vs-pro comparison: the fan left more cultural terms unexplained (*izakaya* -> "sake shop") and used fewer TL notes, assuming a knowledgeable readership. Both translators aimed to keep notes minimal, and O'Hagan judged both strategies valid. — summarized by [Frog-kun, 7 Oct 2015](https://frogkun.com/2015/10/07/translation-theory-for-anime-fans-a-case-study-of-a-fan-translator-vs-a-professional/)
- [Fan opinion] Committee of Zero, a fan VN group, criticized the official *CHAOS;HEAD NOAH* EN for "untranslated Japanese" and translator notes, and for failing "to capture the personality and ambiance of the original". — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1961950/discussions/0/3392923906945820576) (fan opinion)

**Line length, text boxes, technical constraints**
- [Consensus] Japanese is dense. A five-kanji compound can expand to "super incredible, enchanted potion of ultimate revival" and overflow the box (Heemsbergen). On *Half-Minute Hero*, a late font change cut character limits, and every overflowing line had to be revised in a week (Chavez). — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- On *Persona 5*, a recurring problem was lines too long "for the text window or scene timing", on top of lines that "won't make sense... if translated word for word". — Yu Namba, Atlus USA, [PlayStation Blog, 4 Apr 2017](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2017/04/04/how-atlus-has-kept-persona-5s-localisation-authentic-to-the-japanese-original/)
- Engines built for unspaced Japanese may not word-wrap. In RPG Maker MV, English translators add a WordWrap plugin tag to each message. — [itch.io dev post](https://itch.io/post/16189699) (snippet-only). Example HUD limit: 50 characters per line, which English quickly exceeds. — [Crowdin community](https://community.crowdin.com/t/character-limit-before-line-break/7901) (snippet-only). Ren'Py's translation framework allows splitting or merging lines where English sentence breaks differ, plus per-language fonts and images. — [VN Paths guide](https://vnpaths.com/how-to-translate-a-visual-novel/)
- Scale for context: *Yakuza 0* has ~1.8M Japanese characters, against a typical JRPG range of ~1–1.2M. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)

**Ellipses, stutters, punctuation, 「」**
- [Opinion, Fuwanovel] Most VN translations overuse ellipses, and most should be removed. The exception is a trailing ellipsis marking an implied, unspoken line (a "pregnant pause"). — ["Killing the ellipsis in VN translations", Fuwanovel re:Edit blog](https://forums.fuwanovel.moe/blogs/entry/1264-killing-the-ellipsis-%E2%80%9C%E2%80%9D-in-vn-translations/) (snippet-only, 403; date not confirmed)
- [Practice] Kiryu speaks in many ellipses in Japanese (a blank-slate player-identification device). The English gives him more defined lines, because Western players find it harder to project themselves onto a "Japanese badass". — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- [Community style guide] Replace 「」 with English quotation marks and 。/、 with period and comma. Keep special symbols like ♡ as they are. — [Umamusume Wiki guidelines](https://umamusu.wiki/Umamusume_Wiki:Contributing/Guidelines)
- [Academic] Orthographic devices carry voice: a fan translator used stretched vowels ("Reeeally bad", "Okaaay", "I'lllll kill yoooou!") where the professional wrote "Man, I feel sick" / "OK" / "I'll kill you!". This conveyed colloquial speech and the okama stereotype, and O'Hagan judged the fan stronger on genre awareness. — [Frog-kun 2015, summarizing O'Hagan 2008](https://frogkun.com/2015/10/07/translation-theory-for-anime-fans-a-case-study-of-a-fan-translator-vs-a-professional/)

**Profanity / interjections**
- [Opinion, Sega] "Translation is not mathematics", so *kuso* can't just become "damn". Editors in M-rated titles can use a curse if it makes the joke land as hard as the original. A flat 「なに！？」 over a dramatic zoom shot was replaced by a punchier English line matching the shot, which Lee says still honors authorial intent. — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)

### Inferences
(These are my synthesis into line-level rules. Where marked "illustrative", the example renderings are mine, not taken from a published translation.)

**A. Honorific decision tree** (synthesized from Santos & Humblé, ANN, MangaGamer 2019, Strichart, Umamusume Wiki):
1. Japan-set setting (school, office, yakuza, modern gacha such as Uma Musume or Project Sekai) -> keep -san/-chan/-kun/-sama/-senpai/-sensei hyphenated in romaji. Fantasy or non-Japan setting -> replace with English forms of address (Sir, Lady, Master, Miss, Your Highness) or drop them.
2. Whatever the choice, it must be one global, project-wide rule. Never mix "Yuki-chan" in one line and "little Yuki" in the next.
3. When an honorific *shifts* (dropping -san, adding -chan, switching to yobisute), that's a plot beat. If honorifics are kept, the shift is visible. If they were dropped, compensate in that line ("Just call me Haruka." / switching from surname to given name).
4. Family terms such as onii-chan/onee-sama work as names in Japanese. Common options are "Big Brother"/"Bro"/"Sis", "Onii-chan" kept as a name, or "Brother" (formal). Pick by character register: a clingy little sister -> "Big Bro"; an ojousama -> "Elder Sister"/"Onee-sama".
5. Senpai and sensei are now widely recognized in English (Strichart 2018), so keeping them is low-cost. "Aniki" with a one-time gloss beats "sir" (the Yakuza 6 lesson).

**B. Particle -> English function map** (illustrative; derived from the linguistics sources plus common practice, not from a pro style guide). The rule is to render the *function*, never insert a literal word every time.
- ね (seeking agreement/softening): tag question ("..., right?", "..., huh?", "isn't it?"), or nothing plus a warmer word choice. 「いい天気だね」 -> "Nice weather, huh?"
- よ (informing/asserting): "you know", "I'm telling you", an exclamation mark, or nothing. 「知らないよ」 -> "How should I know?" / "I don't know, okay?"
- よね: "..., right?" / "You did, didn't you?"
- わ (feminine, rising): soft, refined word choice. Don't add "dear" to every line. わ (Kansai, falling) belongs to dialect, not femininity.
- ぞ / ぜ (masculine emphasis, bravado): imperative energy, "Let's go!", "I'm gonna...", "Got it?!"
- な / なあ (masculine/casual or musing): "Man...", "Huh.", "I guess..."
- かしら (feminine wondering): "I wonder...", "Might it be...?"
- の (explanation/soft question): 「どうしたの？」 -> "What's wrong?" (gentle). 「行くの」 -> "I'm going, okay?" (soft insistence).
- でしょ / だろ (assumed agreement, sometimes scolding): "Right?", "Told you!", "Obviously!", "C'mon, you know that."
- っす (junior, sporty, casual-polite): peppy contractions plus deference markers ("Yes sir!", "Senpai, check this out!"). Don't render it as a written tic.

**C. Pronoun and role-language compensation toolkit** (from LoL and Heerink, illustrative):
- Ore -> contractions, blunt clauses, mild profanity, "damn", "the hell". Boku (male) -> politer, fuller sentences, hedges ("um", "I think"). Bokukko (boku-girl) -> tomboyish casual voice. Some translators keep a signature line about it if the plot comments on her pronoun.
- Ore-sama -> third-person self-reference or swagger ("The great Papyrus!", "It's ya boy").
- Washi + じゃ (old man/hakase) -> "Hmm, my boy", "Indeed", older-fashioned vocabulary. Avoid broad hillbilly spellings.
- Watakushi + ですわ (ojousama) -> no contractions, elevated vocabulary ("I daresay", "How vulgar"), and keep the 「おーっほっほ」 laugh as "Ohohoho!" since it's a recognizable trope.
- Sessha + でござる (samurai) -> formal, slightly archaic syntax used *sparingly* ("I shall", "This one"). The FFVI "thee/thou" route is considered heavy-handed.
- Royal 余/朕 -> royal "we".
- Kansai -> follow Sega's rule: no fixed Southern-twang mapping. Use a warm, fast, blunt, joking colloquial register. Keep "-han" if honorifics are kept.
- Tics (なのだ, にゃ, っす, ござる, catchphrases) -> choose one English device per character (a fixed tag phrase, a sound such as "nya", a syntactic habit). Record it on the character sheet and apply it at a *controlled frequency* (not every line), since kodack warns invented English tics drift.

**D. Punctuation/orthography rules for VN text** (from Umamusume Wiki, Fuwanovel, O'Hagan; the specifics are conventional practice):
- 「」 -> “ ” ; 『』 -> nested quotes, or italics for titles and inner thoughts; 。、 -> . , ; ！？ -> ?! (English order); ♡/♪ kept if the UI font supports them.
- …… -> a single "..." (not "......"). Cut ellipses that only mark Japanese hesitation rhythm, and keep trailing ones that carry an implied line (Fuwanovel).
- ―― (interruption or trailing off) -> em dash "—", e.g. "Wait, I didn't mean—"
- Stutter 「あ、あの…」 -> "U-um..." ; 「ば、ばか！」 -> "Y-you idiot!" Limit stutters to one per line. Stuttering the first word only is the English VN convention.
- Elongation ー/～ (「お願い～」) -> stretched vowels ("Pleeease") or an exclamation mark, at low frequency.
- Small っ at line end (「あっ」) -> "Ah!" ; 「えっ」 -> "Huh?!"
- Onomatopoeia/SFX (ドキドキ, ゴゴゴ, ニヤリ): in narration, translate to verbs or descriptions ("My heart pounded"; "she grinned slyly"). In SFX-only lines, use English SFX ("Thump thump", "*rumble*"), or keep romaji with a typographic convention if it is baked into art.

**E. Length rule**: English usually runs longer than Japanese in character count (Heemsbergen's kanji-compound example). VN and gacha boxes need the line fitted to the box: cut redundancy (repeated subject or particles) before cutting content, and split one Japanese line into two English lines only where the engine allows it (Ren'Py).

### Gaps
- No published professional style guide (Sega, Atlus, NISA, MangaGamer, JAST, Cygames) with explicit particle-by-particle or stutter/ellipsis rules was retrievable. The Fuwanovel ellipsis post and MangaGamer honorific post returned 403, so their full reasoning is unread.
- No sourced JP->EN text-expansion ratio (e.g. "English is ~X% longer") was found. The only concrete limits are anecdotal (50-char HUD; Netflix's ~42 chars/line for subtitles, which doesn't apply to VNs).
- Not verified this session, though widely cited: *Ace Attorney*'s name puns (Naruhodō -> Phoenix Wright); *Rurouni Kenshin*'s ござる as "that I am/that I do"; *Danganronpa*'s Monokuma laugh うぷぷ -> "Upupu/Puhuhu". Treat these as leads, not facts.
- No game-specific study of SFX/onomatopoeia handling in VN text was found. The SFX rules in Inferences D are conventional practice, not sourced.
- The Leiden thesis on yakuwarigo (Heerink 2018) and Teshigawara & Kinsui (2012) could not be read in full, so their case examples are missing.

## Q2. What is the "literal vs localized" debate (well-known cases, post-mortems), and what balance do respected translators recommend?

### Takeaway
Among professionals the consensus is that "literal translation does not exist". The target is the original's intent, feeling and characterization, normally produced by a literal-leaning translator pass followed by a creative editor pass. The live controversy is about how far *beyond* intent localizers may go: adding jokes, substituting Anglophone stereotypes for Japanese social markers, injecting memes. Japanese-literate fans increasingly push back on that, so the recommended middle is "faithful to function and character, natural in English, no added content".

### Cited Findings
- [Consensus, pro] "Literal translation does not exist because translating is a creative venture" (Derek Heemsbergen, editor on *Dragalia Lost* and *Sakuna*). The goal is preserving intent and feeling, not one-to-one word swaps (John Ricciardi, 8-4). Intent and meaning take priority, because a literal version loses what the original team wanted players to feel (Jessica Chavez, ex-Xseed). — [Game Informer, 24 Nov 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- [Consensus, pro] "A direct translation, that's not a localization", because literal text reads dry. The Yakuza workflow is: translator gives a close, literal draft, then the editor makes it natural English. "The humor of a Yakuza game is a fine line to walk." — Scott Strichart, [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west). The literal draft is "accurate" but "clunky, dry", and editors add characterization. — Marilyn Lee, [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- [Pro, counterweight toward foreignness] Strichart says Yakuza players should get a somewhat foreign experience, and the game should still feel Japanese. — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization). Lee says localizers must convey politically charged text accurately, so "players have the freedom to come to their own conclusions." — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- [Academic, trend] Japanese game localization has moved toward less cultural adaptation over time. — [Mangiron, "Found in Translation: Evolving Approaches for the Localization of Japanese Video Games"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348781368_Found_in_Translation_Evolving_Approaches_for_the_Localization_of_Japanese_Video_Games) (snippet-only; year not confirmed). Earlier framing from Mangiron & O'Hagan: game localization aims to reproduce the *play experience*, which gives translators more latitude than literary or film work. — [ResearchGate, "Putting Pleasure First"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314515972_Putting_Pleasure_First_Localizing_Japanese_Video_Games) (snippet-only). Santos & Humblé (2023) see *Yakuza 0*'s retained honorifics as evidence of rising audience tolerance for foreignizing translation. — [Cadernos de Tradução](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/7319/731976434018/movil/)
- **Case: *Persona 5* (2017)** [Contested]. Some professional JP->EN translators called the dialogue "stilted and unnatural" with awkward grammar. Futaba's "-channer" slang ("kek") was mocked. Other fans defended a more literal approach to preserve cultural flavor, explicitly against the "liberal twists" of *Fire Emblem Fates* ("Now you know how we felt about Fire Emblem Fates"). The reviewer blamed missing context checks (translators working from raw text) and a compressed schedule. The script was ~50% larger than *P4G* and used more translators and editors than any previous Atlus USA project. — [Destructoid, Josh Tolentino, 5 Apr 2017](https://www.destructoid.com/some-fans-are-pissed-about-persona-5s-localization/). Atlus's own line: "something... simply won't make sense to the Western audience if translated word for word". — Yu Namba, [PlayStation Blog, 4 Apr 2017](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2017/04/04/how-atlus-has-kept-persona-5s-localisation-authentic-to-the-japanese-original/). Sam Mullen later said translation had to start mid-development, and two different writers on one character will sound different. — [Push Square, Jan 2019](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2019/01/it_sounds_like_persona_5_was_a_bit_of_a_nightmare_to_localise) (snippet-only; attribution via search summary)
- **Case: *Ys VIII* (2017–18)**, a quality post-mortem rather than a literal-vs-free one. Typos, grammar errors, stilted dialogue and an apparent lack of editing led NIS America's CEO Takuro Yamashita to formally apologize on 10 Oct 2017. A new translator and editor were brought in, and a free patch on 30 Jan 2018 rewrote most of the text with an almost fully re-recorded dub. — [Niche Gamer, 10 Oct 2017](https://nichegamer.com/2017/10/10/nis-america-apologies-for-localization/); [RPG Site](https://www.rpgsite.net/news/6187-upcoming-patch-for-ys-viii-lacrimosa-of-dana-will-include-nearly-100-re-write-of-voiced-lines)
- **Case: *Umamusume* EN (Global launch June 2025; CEDEC 2026 talk)** [Contested]. As summarized from slides by a critic, Cygames separates "translation" (moving information) from "localization" (recreating a character's essence via dialects, internet communities, period slang, memes and invented quirks), and "created unique speech styles and quirks for each character". The critic documents an expanded gift line turned into a Santa-style line, 真剣勝負やぞ omitted for Tamamo, たまらん turned into an added "fanservice" reference for Agnes, and "Let her cook"/"Umasta-worthy" added for Helios. The critic argues that awards, polish and consistency don't prove fidelity and calls for "faithful English with restrained compensation". — [Localizers Exposed, 15 Aug 2026](https://localizers.ch/column/when-localization-stops-translating-what-cygames-cedec-2026-umamusume-presentation-actually-shows/) (critic's site with an explicit stance; read as one side)
- **Case: *Judgment* (Ryan Acosta)**: a literal rendering of a foreign character's Japanese would have sounded like an *SMT* angel, so the editor gave him a fitting "weeb" personality instead. This is localized characterization justified by intent. — Josh Malone (Sega/Atlus), [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- **Case: CHAOS;HEAD NOAH**: the fan group Committee of Zero argued the official EN lost the original's "personality and ambiance" and objected to untranslated Japanese and TL notes. This criticism comes from the "too stiff/too foreign" direction, the reverse of the Umamusume critique. — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1961950/discussions/0/3392923906945820576) (fan opinion)
- [Fan-TL view] Literal vs localized is a team-level choice that depends on "the material and the intended audience", and dialogue "must flow naturally when read at the pace a visual novel delivers it". — [VN Paths guide](https://vnpaths.com/how-to-translate-a-visual-novel/)
- [Academic] Persona fandom members are more critical of JP->EN translations because they know some Japanese language and culture. — [Academia.edu: "Should we translate for the fans? ... Persona 5"](https://www.academia.edu/42171381/Should_we_translate_for_the_fans_An_audience_response_analysis_study_of_the_localisation_of_the_Japanese_roleplaying_game_Persona_5) (snippet-only)

### Inferences
- The debate has two distinct failure modes. **Under-localization**, where the text is literal but stilted (*Ys VIII*, parts of *P5*), is judged a *quality* failure by nearly everyone. **Over-localization**, where content is added or Japanese markers are swapped for Anglophone stereotypes or memes (*Fire Emblem Fates* per fans, *Umamusume* per its critic), is a *fidelity* failure contested mainly by Japanese-literate fans. The pro consensus quotes (Heemsbergen, Ricciardi, Chavez, Strichart, Lee) all reject the first mode. The Umamusume critique targets the second.
- A defensible "faithful but natural" line, usable as LLM prompt rules:
  1. Every line must carry the source's *information, intent, emotional temperature, register and characterization*.
  2. Wording, syntax and idiom may change freely to achieve that.
  3. Don't add jokes, memes, pop-culture references or new information absent from the source. Rebuilding a pun is allowed only where the source has one (Yakuza "safe sects").
  4. Don't omit clauses (cf. the 真剣勝負やぞ omission).
  5. Keep Japan-anchored social markers (honorifics, senpai, school and workplace hierarchy) in Japan-set works rather than substituting American equivalents.
  6. Express dialect, role language and tics through register, not a real-world English accent.
  7. Avoid time-bound internet slang ("kek", "let her cook"), which dates quickly and drew backlash in *P5*.
- Sega's two-stage translator-then-editor pipeline is the professional template. An LLM pipeline can mimic it with a literal-gloss pass followed by a voice/naturalness pass constrained by a character sheet.

### Gaps
- Fire Emblem Fates (2016) and other famous cases (Xenoblade X, Trails/NISA honorifics, Nier) were not fetched. Their specifics (e.g. the Soleil support rewrite) rest only on the P5 fans' passing comparison here.
- Cygames' own CEDEC 2026 slides and any rebuttal weren't seen. All Umamusume details come via a hostile summary.
- I found no recent (2023–26) essay from a major professional VN translator (MangaGamer, JAST, Sekai Project, Shiravune) laying out a house philosophy.

## Q3. Which style guides and glossary practices do professional localizers use (character bibles, voice sheets, term bases, consistency checks), and how do fan-TL groups for VNs organize this?

### Takeaway
Professional teams lock key terms up front in a glossary or term base, keep per-character voice guidelines, query the developer for context, and give a lead editor or producer the final pass so every character keeps one voice. The biggest documented failure is lost context and divergent voices when several writers share one character. Fan-TL groups copy this structure with lighter tooling: shared spreadsheets, a glossary, a style guide, and translator/editor/hacker/beta-reader roles.

### Cited Findings
- [Pro, Atlus USA] *Persona 5* used the most translators and editors of any Atlus localization. The team settled "key terms and ideas" at the start so everyone shared guidelines, and the project lead made final calls on "what to keep, what to cut out, what to change, what are the consequences". — Yu Namba, [PlayStation Blog, 4 Apr 2017](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2017/04/04/how-atlus-has-kept-persona-5s-localisation-authentic-to-the-japanese-original/)
- [Pro, Sega/Atlus] Strichart acts as the final consistency pass and sets how characters sound. He reportedly revised ~80% of one editor's text on *Yakuza 6* to match Kiryu's voice. Documented consistency failures include inconsistent capitalization and spelling, and two editors giving the same British-accented *Radiant Historia* character different accents. More contributors means more consistency risk. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- [Pro, context] "Localization teams need context; that's 100 percent of our job." Yakuza localization started before half the game was built, so the team asked developers about character positioning and tone. The process is now collaborative rather than text "thrown over a wall". — Strichart, [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization). The team also asked developers whether a subtle Majima–Nishitani parallel was intended, and they approved making it slightly clearer. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- [Pro, voice work] Even minor characters (Tom, the Mysterious Merchant in *Ishin!*) got days of work on distinct voices. The period setting was supported by an updated glossary plus "astute translation and sharp editing". — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- [Pro, gacha] Cygames *Umamusume* EN (as summarized from CEDEC 2026 slides by a critic): translators were assigned characters they already liked. Each character has guidelines covering overview, speech/dialect notes and glossary. LQA checks readability and consistency, and a Slack channel is used for experimentation (e.g. brainstorming English nicknames for ゴルピッピ, such as "Gold Spice"). — [Localizers Exposed, 15 Aug 2026](https://localizers.ch/column/when-localization-stops-translating-what-cygames-cedec-2026-umamusume-presentation-actually-shows/)
- [Pro, voice rule example] A concrete per-character rule from NISA: elder Roselia gets limited contractions in casual scenes and none in formal ones. — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- [Pro, reference] Give translators speaker details or pre-specified pronouns to avoid flattening and inconsistency. — [Legends of Localization 2019](https://legendsoflocalization.com/articles/personal-pronouns-in-japanese/)
- [Vendor practice] Glossaries cover "what do we call this" (names, items, terms) and style guides cover "how should this sound" (register, formatting). Loc kits for narrative games include character profiles (age, attitude, way of speaking, verbal tics), relationship maps and screenshots, because a string without context could be a combat prompt, a loading screen or dialogue. Translation memory keeps repeated strings consistent. — attributed by search summary to [LCP Localizations](https://lcplocalizations.com/blog/the-importance-of-style-guides-and-glossaries-in-game-localization), [Superjump](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/unlocking-global-markets-a-guide-to-localizing-game-characters/), [Keywords Studios](https://www.keywordsstudios.com/en/about-us/news-events/news/a-step-by-step-guide-to-game-localization/) (vendor blogs; individual attribution not verified)
- [Fan-TL] Roles are: translator ("cannot be substituted"); editor (makes text "feel written, not converted"); hacker or technical lead (extraction and reinsertion, "often the bottleneck"); image editors for text in CGs and UI; and community beta readers who play the patch and flag issues. Teams keep "technical terms, location names, and recurring phrases" in "a shared glossary" and a style guide, and use spreadsheets so multiple translators can work in parallel. Editing catches names or terms that differ between sections or contradict earlier choices. Any MT draft must be "reviewed and revised thoroughly". — [VN Paths, "How to Translate a Visual Novel"](https://vnpaths.com/how-to-translate-a-visual-novel/) (undated, ~2024–26)
- [Fan community style guide, gacha] The Umamusume Wiki fixes official terms first (racing strategies "Runaway / Front Runner / Pace Chaser / Late Surger / End Closer", "Signature Racewear (Shoubufuku)", "Tenno Sho"), sets romanization rules (single vowel for long vowels, は/を/へ -> wa/o/e), bans copying raw MT, and bans AI-generated content. — [Umamusume Wiki guidelines](https://umamusu.wiki/Umamusume_Wiki:Contributing/Guidelines)
- [Academic] Fan VN translators are inherently "visible" (2026 conference abstract using Ace Attorney as a case study). — [UCF ELO 2026](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/elo2026/hypertextsandfictions/schedule/19) (abstract only)

### Inferences
- A minimal "loc kit" for an LLM or human VN/gacha pipeline, assembled from these sources:
  1. **Term base**: names in fixed romanization and name order; skills/items/places; recurring phrases and catchphrases with one approved rendering each.
  2. **Character voice sheet** per speaker: age, role, pronoun (俺/僕/私…) and the English device chosen for it; politeness default and to whom they use keigo; particle/tic habits and the English device plus its frequency; contraction policy; profanity ceiling; dialect treatment; how they address each other character (honorific matrix: who calls whom what).
  3. **Global style rules**: honorific policy, punctuation mapping (「」/……/――), SFX policy, TL-note policy, line-length limits per text box.
  4. **Context**: scene summary and speaker tags, which address the "context is 100% of our job" point.
  5. **Consistency QA**: a final single-voice pass by one lead (the Strichart model) plus glossary-diff checks.
- The *Radiant Historia* and *P5* cases show that multi-writer drift is the main risk in long VNs and gacha event stories. A single canonical voice sheet per character is the key countermeasure. For LLM translation, this means sending the voice sheet with every chunk rather than relying on earlier context.

### Gaps
- No actual internal style guide or character bible from a JP->EN studio was retrievable. Descriptions are second-hand, from interviews and vendor blogs.
- Detailed fan-TL organizational documents (TLWiki-era guides, Committee of Zero or Mirror Moon style guides, roles such as TLC/QC) weren't found online in this session. The searches turned up tools rather than published guides.

## Q4. Concrete good vs bad renderings of typical VN/gacha lines (tsundere, keigo maid, chuunibyou, child speech, rough delinquent speech, etc.)

### Takeaway
Few sources print side-by-side JP/EN line comparisons. The documented examples mostly show *functional* re-creation: pun rebuilt, tic replaced, register shown through contractions, persona amplified. The archetype examples below are illustrative constructions that apply those documented principles, and they are labeled as such.

### Cited Findings (documented examples)
- **Pun**: 布団が吹っ飛んだ (Yakuza minigame) -> "How do you avoid dangerous cults? Practice safe sects!" Good: it keeps the *function* of a groan-pun, not its content. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- **Term of address**: アニキ -> "sir" (criticized as bad, losing the yakuza brotherhood nuance) -> later "aniki" plus a one-time gloss (good). — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- **Silent protagonist ellipses**: Kiryu's many 「……」 in Japanese become fuller, defined English lines. — [Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)
- **Interjection over a dramatic shot**: a bare 「なに！？」 ("What!?") was replaced with an English line matched to an over-the-top zoom. Kuso ≠ automatic "damn". — [Kotaku 2023](https://kotaku.com/yakuza-like-a-dragon-ishin-majima-kiryu-localization-1850263200)
- **Archaic elder voice**: "old-timey accent" (rejected after table read) -> contraction control (adopted). — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- **Foreigner's Japanese**: a literal rendering would make Ryan Acosta sound like an SMT angel, so he got a fitting "weeb" English voice instead. — [Game Informer 2021](https://gameinformer.com/2021/11/24/the-trials-and-tribulations-of-video-game-localization)
- **Ore-sama swagger**: "it's ya boy Guzma". **Royal yo**: royal "we" (King of All Cosmos). — [LoL comments 2019](https://legendsoflocalization.com/articles/personal-pronouns-in-japanese/)
- **Colloquial/effeminate voice via orthography**: "I'lllll kill yoooou!" (fan) vs "I'll kill you!" (pro). O'Hagan judged the fan version more genre-aware. — [Frog-kun 2015 / O'Hagan 2008](https://frogkun.com/2015/10/07/translation-theory-for-anime-fans-a-case-study-of-a-fan-translator-vs-a-professional/)
- **Rural speech (contested)**: Yukino Bijin's どうすりゃいいンだぁ ("what am I supposed to do?") -> "okey dokey / in the soup" (1930s–60s Americana). The critic calls it substitution, not translation. **Kansai (contested)**: Tamamo Cross gets eye-dialect spelling ("dis"), and the clause 真剣勝負やぞ ("this is a serious race") was dropped. **Gyaru (contested)**: いい波乗ってんね ("you're riding a good wave") with added "Let her cook" / "Umasta-worthy". — [Localizers Exposed 2026](https://localizers.ch/column/when-localization-stops-translating-what-cygames-cedec-2026-umamusume-presentation-actually-shows/)
- **Internet slang backlash**: Futaba's "-channer" English ("kek") in *P5* was widely mocked. — [Destructoid 2017](https://www.destructoid.com/some-fans-are-pissed-about-persona-5s-localization/)
- **Tic**: だってばよ -> "Believe it!" (dub, lip-sync driven, later dropped), then "ya know". — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/what-does-dattebayo-mean-in-naruto/)
- **Samurai ござる**: FFVI Cyan's "thee/thou" English, judged a poor fit by fan commentary. — [Tropedia](https://tropedia.fandom.com/wiki/Verbal_Tic) (weak source)
- **Ojousama background**: the term implies a rich, sheltered and often naive girl, which matters for tone. — [Japanese with Anime, 20 May 2018](https://www.japanesewithanime.com/2018/05/ojousama.html)

### Inferences (illustrative renderings I constructed by applying the documented principles; not from published translations)
- **Tsundere**: 「べ、別にあんたのために作ったんじゃないんだからね！」
  - Bad (stiff literal): "It is not that I made it for you specially, you know!"
  - Trope-literal (acceptable for an anime-savvy audience, but now a cliché): "I-it's not like I made it for you or anything!"
  - Natural (keeps stutter, denial and embarrassment): "D-don't get the wrong idea! I just made too much, that's all!"
  - Rule: keep one stutter on the first word. The ね/んだから insistence becomes an exclamation plus "that's all/okay?". あんた becomes the rude-familiar tone, not a word.
- **Keigo maid**: 「お帰りなさいませ、ご主人様。お食事になさいますか、それともお風呂になさいますか？」
  - Bad: "Welcome back home, Master-sama. Will you do the meal, or will you do the bath?"
  - Good: "Welcome home, Master. Shall I serve dinner, or would you prefer to bathe first?"
  - Rule: keigo becomes no contractions plus "shall/would you prefer/if it pleases you". ご主人様 becomes "Master" (don't double up as "Master-sama").
- **Chuunibyou**: 「我が右腕に封印されし漆黒の炎よ……今こそ目覚めよ！」
  - Bad (flattened): "The black fire in my right arm, wake up now!"
  - Good: "O jet-black flame sealed within my right arm... now, awaken!"
  - Rule: the comedy *is* the overwrought register. Keep archaic vocatives, inverted syntax and grandiose vocabulary, and don't normalize it. 我 becomes a grand "I" or a third-person title if the character has one.
- **Child speech**: 「あのね、おにいちゃん！きょうね、ようちえんでね、おえかきしたの！」
  - Bad: "Listen, elder brother. Today at kindergarten I did a drawing."
  - Good: "Big Brother, guess what! Today at kindergarten, I drew a picture, and...!"
  - Rule: the hiragana-only childishness becomes simple words, run-on "and... and...", and "guess what". Avoid misspellings ("pwease") unless the source itself lisps.
- **Delinquent/yankee**: 「てめぇ、どこ見てんだコラァ！」
  - Bad: "You, where are you looking? Hey!"
  - Good: "The hell're you lookin' at, huh?!"
  - Rule: てめぇ/コラ become aggressive contractions plus a profanity level per rating. Drop g's sparingly, and keep the stretched ァ as "huh?!" energy rather than spelling it out.
- **Ojousama**: 「このわたくしに勝とうだなんて、百年早いですわ！おーっほっほっほ！」
  - Good: "You? Defeat *me*? Not in a hundred years, darling! Ohohoho!"
  - Rule: わたくし/ですわ become elevated diction with no contractions. The laugh is a genre signal, so keep it.
- **っす kouhai**: 「先輩、それマジやばいっす！」 -> "Senpai, that's seriously wild!" (deferential address plus peppy casual; no literal "-ssu").
- **Old sage**: 「ふぉっふぉっふぉ、若いのう」 -> "Ho ho ho... ah, to be young." (washi/のう become a gentle archaic cadence, not hillbilly spelling).

### Gaps
- No source with authentic side-by-side JP/official-EN/fan-EN comparisons for tsundere, maid, chuunibyou or child archetypes in a VN or gacha game was retrieved. Legends of Localization's game-by-game comparisons are the best candidate for real examples but were not fetched beyond the pronoun article.
- No sourced example yet of how a major gacha (FGO NA, Granblue Fantasy, Project Sekai EN, Uma Musume) renders tics like 〜なのだ or にゃ.
